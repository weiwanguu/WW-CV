High-strength liquid metal composite–hydrogel interfaces enable robust stretchable electronics

Abstract

Stretchable conductors are essential building blocks for next-generation wearable electronics and soft robotics. Among them, liquid metal-based conductors offer exceptional deformability but suffer from poor interfacial adhesion to substrates, often resulting in leakage under mechanical stress that compromises electromechanical stability and device durability. Here we report a universal interface-fusion printing strategy for fabricating metal-particle semi-embedded hydrogels, in which interconnected liquid metal and silver particles are firmly anchored at the hydrogel surface. The resulting liquid metal-based composite layer achieves a high interfacial adhesion strength of 234.4 kPa to the hydrogel substrate and a conductivity of 1.18 × 106 S m−1. This robust interface prevents liquid metal leakage and ensures stable electrical connection under extreme conditions, including prolonged ultrasonication, 300 MPa impacts, and thousands of stretching cycles. The strategy forms an interpenetrating cross-linked polymer network through cross-interfacial assembly, fusing the circuit and substrate into an integrated structure. This simple and scalable method enables the fabrication of high-resolution circuits for a wide range of electronic devices. We demonstrate its performance in applications including stretchable circuits, on-skin biosensors, and underwater soft robots.

Key Highlights

  • A universal interface-fusion printing strategy is developed to fabricate metal-particle semi-embedded hydrogels (MPEH), in which interconnected liquid metal and silver particles are firmly anchored at the hydrogel surface through an interpenetrating cross-linked PVA network.
  • The MPEH achieves an interfacial adhesion strength of 234.4 kPa and a conductivity of 1.18 × 106 S m−1, while maintaining robust electrical performance under prolonged ultrasonication, repeated 300 MPa impacts, thousands of stretching cycles, and large tensile deformation.
  • The strategy enables high-resolution stretchable circuits and is demonstrated in functional circuits, epidermal biosensors, and an underwater hydrogel jellyfish robot with visual feedback during locomotion.

Overview of MPEH

Fig. 1 | Overview of the metal-particle semi-embedded hydrogel (MPEH). a Schematic illustration of the components and the fabrication process of MPEH. The flexible mask used for patterning was fabricated from 300 µm-thick thermoplastic polyurethane via laser engraving. b Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) image showing the semi-embedded structure of LM/Ag particles in MPEH. Scale bar, 400 µm. c Cross-sectional SEM image of MPEH showing seamless integration of the LM/Ag circuit with the PVA hydrogel substrate, with no visible gap. The LM/Ag particles form a stable conductive pathway. Scale bar, 50 µm. d Optical images of the ‘GEL’ LED array integrated on the MPEH before and after stretching to 200% strain. Scale bar, 1 cm. e Optical image of various conductive patterns, including snowflake, dumbbell, circular, and linear geometries fabricated on the MPEH. Scale bar, 1 cm. f Optical image showing the MPEH-based photoplethysmography (PPG) sensor conformally adhering to human skin, maintaining stable and intimate contact during skin deformation. Scale bar, 1 cm. g Optical image of the hydrogel jellyfish robot during underwater locomotion. The jellyfish body, made of PVA hydrogel, was integrated with MPEH-based circuits and LEDs, with LED colour indicating swimming speed. The high transparency, stretchability, and underwater durability of the MPEH enable stable and intuitive visualisation of output signals. Scale bar, 5 cm.

Interface-Fusion Printing Strategy

Fig. 2 | Mechanism of the interface-fusion printing strategy and interfacial robustness of the MPEH. a Schematic illustration of the fabrication process of the MPEH. The formation of an interpenetrating PVA crystalline network between the LM/Ag circuit and the substrate affords strong interfacial adhesion and enhances the mechanical stability of the circuit. b Optical and laser confocal microscopy images of the ink–hydrogel interface before and after drying. They reveal the distribution of PVA chains originating from both the composite ink and the hydrogel substrate. Drying promotes interdiffusion and entanglement of PVA chains, resulting in an interfacial overlap approximately 60 µm thick. The PVA in the ink was grafted with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) groups, while the PVA in the hydrogel substrate was grafted with rhodamine B isothiocyanate (RBITC) groups. FITC emits green fluorescence under 488 nm excitation, while RBITC emits red fluorescence under 561 nm excitation. c Wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) patterns of the PVA hydrogel substrate before and after drying. d SEM images of the cross-section and surface of the MPEH. LM/Ag particles are partially embedded in the hydrogel surface, and the conductive layer is seamlessly integrated with the substrate. Scale bar, 100 µm. e Micro-CT image showing the uniform semi-embedded structure of the MPEH over a macroscopic area of 4 mm × 4 mm. Scale bar, 400 µm. f Lap-shear test setup (left) and measured interfacial shear strength (right) of the LM/Ag/PVA conductive layer on different types of flexible substrates. Inset: optical image illustrating the robust interface of the MPEH. Scale bar, 1 cm. Data are presented as mean values ± standard deviation (n = 3). g Comparison of interfacial adhesion strength and electrical conductivity of various conductive materials on different substrates. References are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Mechanical and Electrical Properties

Fig. 3 | Mechanical and electrical properties and stability of MPEH. a Optical images of the LM/Ag/PVA conductive layer on different substrates before and after stretching. Scale bar, 1 cm. b Finite element analysis simulation showing stress distribution under stretching at varying interfacial bonding strengths between the conductive layer and the substrate. c Optical images comparing the mechanical integrity of the MPEH and a LM-coated hydrogel (LCH) before and after 60 W ultrasonication. The LCH was fabricated by stencil-printing bulk liquid metal onto a PVA hydrogel substrate. Scale bar, 1 cm. d Surface resistance of the MPEH and the LCH as a function of ultrasonication time. Measurements were performed using a Keithley DMM6500 digital multimeter in a four-terminal configuration to ensure accuracy and minimise external interference. Data are presented as mean values ± standard deviation (n = 3). e Resistance changes of the MPEH and the LCH during repetitive impact testing, in which a 100 g ball was dropped from a height of 1 m, generating an impact stress of 300 MPa per strike. Curves of f, stress-strain and g conductivity and elongation at break of MPEH with varying LM/Ag particle loading. Data are presented as mean values ± standard deviation (n = 3). h Relative resistance change as a function of uniaxial tensile strain for MPEH with varying LM/Ag particle loading, along with a theoretical prediction based on bulk conductor assumptions (dashed line). i Relative resistance change of MPEH over 1000 stretching cycles under 100% strain. j Contact resistance between MPEH conductive traces and metal pins of electronic components measured using the transmission line method. Linear fitting yields a contact resistance (Rc) of 0.03 Ω (R2 = 0.995). Inset: schematic illustration of the sample layout for contact resistance measurement.

Functional Circuits

Fig. 4 | Functional circuits based on MPEH. a Schematic illustration of the assembly of flexible functional circuits. b Optical images of a complex multi-LED circuit in the flat state (top) and conformally wrapped around a wrist (bottom). The circuit exhibits a high integration density of 4 electronic components per square centimetre while retaining good flexibility and conformal contact with the skin. c Optical images of a dual-LED stroboscopic circuit operating at a frequency of 1.46 Hz. The circuit consists of four resistors, one capacitor, two LEDs, and an NE555N integrated circuit (IC). d A demonstration of the stroboscopic circuit retaining functional integrity under three mechanical deformations: (i) uniaxial stretching, (ii) biaxial stretching, and (iii) folding. e A miniaturised LED circuit integrated on a fingertip. f Photograph illustrating the stable operation of an MPEH-based LED circuit after 12 hours of water immersion. The circuit’s underwater stability arises from the anti-swelling properties of the PVA hydrogel substrate and the inherent hydrophobicity of the LM/Ag particles. Scale bars, 1 cm.

Epidermal Biosensors

Fig. 5 | Epidermal biosensors based on MPEH. a Diverse epidermal sensors for acquiring a broad spectrum of physiological signals, including human motion, cardiac pulse, and electromyographic (EMG) activity. b Optical image of a strain sensor placed on the index finger. A strain-sensing layer composed of silver microflakes was located in Region 2 (R2). Mechanical deformation induced microcracks in the layer, resulting in pronounced changes in electrical resistance. Sensor resistance was measured using a digital multimetre (Keithley DMM6500). Scale bar, 1 cm. c Resistance response curves of the strain sensor during bending at Region 1 (R1) and Region 2 (R2). R1 contained no silver-based strain element, and the MPEH-based circuit maintained stable resistance during deformation, ensuring that the output signal remained unaffected by mechanical disturbances in non-sensing areas. d Optical image of a photoplethysmography (PPG) sensor placed on the wrist. The circuit design is detailed in the Methods section. Output voltage signals were recorded using an oscilloscope. Scale bar, 1 cm. e Real-time pulse waveforms captured by the PPG sensor. f Optical image of three strip-shaped MPEH bioelectrodes conformally attached to the forearm. The ionically conductive PVA hydrogel substrate adhered to the skin, whereas the electronically conductive layer interfaced with metal clips, establishing a seamless ion-to-electron conduction pathway between the skin and external electronic devices. g EMG signals recorded from the bioelectrodes under varying grip strengths.

Underwater Jellyfish Robot

Fig. 6 | MPEH-based jellyfish robot. a Simplified block diagram of the circuit design for the jellyfish robot. Underwater motion commands are transmitted from a computer through a wireless communication module. Powered by a compact, constant-voltage 5 V lithium-ion battery, the jellyfish robot employs a control system to regulate motor speed and the on/off state of the two LEDs. b Optical image of the jellyfish robot. The power supply, control-system PCB, motor-driver PCB, and wireless communication module are housed within the head shell. A motor, linked to the six tentacles via a transmission mechanism, controls the swimming speed. The body, fabricated from PVA hydrogel, replicates the morphology of a real jellyfish. An MPEH-based circuit bearing two LEDs is mounted on the hydrogel body. Scale bar, 5 cm. c Real-time optical images of the jellyfish robot swimming upward and downward. Owing to the high transparency of the MPEH and the circuit’s underwater mechanical stability, swimming speed is intuitively indicated in real time via LED colour changes: blue indicates fast swimming, while green indicates slow swimming. Scale bar, 10 cm. d Displacement-time curve of the jellyfish robot during swimming.
